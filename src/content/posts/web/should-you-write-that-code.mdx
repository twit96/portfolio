  ## The Before Times

  In the before times (the period between college and stable full-time employment),
  I was eager to program more and more. It was all I could do.
  After all, there's so much to learn and I felt so behind, and it's so fun!

  Bright eyed and bushy-tailed, I put together numerous projects with React, Astro, 
  Laravel, WordPress, and God only knows what else. I was learning so much! I was unstoppable!
  I was making mistakes and fixing them, and creating more mistakes with my fixes,
  then really (truly) fixing them for good this time (I promise).

  It was addicting, and rewarding to see my code turn into something tangible and public.
  Something that I could send to my friends and family so they could open it on their phones
  (after all, the frontends were <em>always</em> mobile-first fluid layouts).

  Eventually, I got a stable position working in Enterprise Systems.
  Work consisted of exactly what I'd been wanting to work on professionally - lots of programming 
  and troubleshooting across numerous systems with numerous different technologies.
  An ability to focus on architecture in a real world scenario.
  And we were even going through an ERP conversion. How cool, right? Right?!

</PostSection>
<PostSection>

  ## The Crazy Ex

  But as time went on, my ex, GitHub Dependabot, began reaching out. 
  During my workdays, on the weekends, before bedtime. 
  Constantly reminding me of the obligations I left behind.

  I did my best to keep up, focusing on a handful of key projects to keep up-to-date outside of work.
  Then the vulnerabilities and exploits struck. Now my ex got the friends involved,
  and they were ALL reaching out. Web hosting and deployment vendors, 
  server provisioning and management, database hosting platforms - letting me know 
  they needed me right now.

  And nevermind various updated terms of service notifications, through which my inaction implies agreement.
  Who knows what horrors I've agreed to by putting yet another email from an old project's software vendor 
  into the trash on a Tuesday after dinner.

</PostSection>
<PostSection>

  ## Attempted Compromises

  In questionable judgment, I reworked one of my active websites from purely static 
  to headless with a CMS so I no longer had to perform every content edit for the client. 
  A fresh Payload CMS implementation with Railway in September 2026 (yay!), 
  followed promptly by a Railway security breach in October 2026 requiring me to rotate all credentials (oh).
  So, I traded content ownership for server ownership, and I'm not yet sure how I feel about it 
  (love me a good headless frontend though).

  I've also paid to keep an old Laravel proof-of-concept website live for the past 3 years  as a fun portfolio piece, 
  but October 2026's PHP2Shell vulnerability made sure the project had my undivided attention after work for a couple days. 
  Even my church's WordPress website begged for some attention that week.

  Today (making the most of my relaxation time off of work, of course),
  I revisited a static React frontend project I last touched two years ago to update its dependencies.
  I even brought the dependency count from 25 down to 9 (cleaning up from a long-gone codebase refactor). 
  However, I never got to play with new features or obsess over architecture during the session, 
  which is what makes programming so fun! Instead, it was all lectures. 
  "We used to spend hours together" this. "Backdoor was unlocked" that. Ugh!

  ## I'm Tired of This, Grandpa

  At this point, it's near impossible to keep up with all of my exes and their friends outside of my day job. 
  I've been making an effort for them for the past few years outside of work,
  often at the expense of my real life and my real relationships.
  So it's time to start breaking it off with some of them (the projects, to be clear).

  The problem is, I love them. The fact that they're live and deployed, and mine, is a big source of meaning to me.
  Seeing how they've grown, and knowing where they could go (if I could jusssst clear the backlog of infinite scope creep),
  has made me so attached to them. Almost as if they're a part of me.
  Who am I when the links are no longer live on my portfolio website?

  Drama aside, I have to be honest with myself. I don't have the time or energy to maintain all of my past projects outside of work.
  If that means taking deployments offline or archiving codebases, then that's okay.

</PostSection>
<PostSection>

  ## Your Software is a Relationship

  My lesson learned the hard way here is that in software development (and Enterprise Systems),
  the first question to ask is not if you <em>can</em> implement the custom solution,
  but rather whether or not you <em>should</em> in the first place. 
  An implementation is not the end of the story. It's a baby that you must now feed and bathe and care for.
  Or if you'd rather, it's a new relationship that requires your attention and care from then on.

  Even the lowest maintenance code will inevitably ask something of you one day.
  Do you (or does your team) have the capacity to keep up with the shiny new play toy?
  Writing and refactoring the codebase and playing with different architectures is fun,
  but are you prepared and able to maintain the solution in the long-term?

</PostSection>
